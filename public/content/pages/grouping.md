From the previous lesson we know that there is a **hierarchy** of objects where we divide into large medium and small shapes.

In the image below, it seems that we have managed to divide everything into different sizes. However, there is some structure missing:

![](https://ik.imagekit.io/71jz3gcgav/image_whi0kUF5U.png "w=250")

So we'll group them by color:
![](https://ik.imagekit.io/71jz3gcgav/image_BHbxQ4hkI.png "w=250")

Now we have a structure. We grouped by color and now the composition looks more cohesive. We like to structure everything and see order. But you can leave room for exceptions to the rules (a little chaos in the midst of order).

For example, among all the red books, add one yellow book, or you can rotate one book a bit in this way:
![](https://ik.imagekit.io/71jz3gcgav/image_k2jbKAvQe.png "w=250")

Let's imagine we're designing a book cover. It looks chaotic here, too:
![](https://ik.imagekit.io/71jz3gcgav/image_Ed6N89c_m.png "w=250")
Of course there are exceptions to the rules. If the goal is to make total chaos, it's not a mistake.

Or we can group some elements together, and now it looks better:
![](https://ik.imagekit.io/71jz3gcgav/image_xb1ukbUHZ.png "w=250")

Grouping is more about simplification, where there is a structure, it is clear where one group of objects starts and where another ends. This principle can be used not to overload the composition with a lot of details.

Example:

You don't always have to group them by type or color. You can group them by size, or by combining them into one shape and one composition:
![](https://ik.imagekit.io/71jz3gcgav/image_mbp7caZBo.png "w=250")

Grouping can also be done by individual semantic sections.
